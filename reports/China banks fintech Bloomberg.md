# China’s fintech interface still rides banks

**Executive conclusion.** China did not replace conventional banking with fintech between 2010 and 2026; it reorganised finance into a layered stack. Fintech platforms captured the customer’s first click, QR code, small-ticket payment frequency, product discovery and much behavioural data, while licensed banks retained deposits, most funded credit, settlement liquidity, custody, capital and large-ticket corporate finance. There is therefore no single, non-overlapping “bank versus fintech transaction share”: the same purchase can be initiated in Alipay, funded from an ICBC deposit, cleared by NetsUnion and settled through the banking system. The most defensible mechanical index shows non-bank payment institutions at **80.6% of the combined bank-electronic/non-bank transaction count but only 8.5% of value in 2025**; the corresponding **2026 H1 actual** split was **80.0% of count and 7.6% of value**, but both calculations deliberately combine overlapping PBOC series and are not ownership shares. At the visible wallet layer, Alipay plus Tenpay/WeChat Pay held roughly **90–95% of third-party mobile-payment activity from the late 2010s through 2026**, while at the balance-sheet layer banking assets rose from **RMB95.3tn in 2010 to RMB480.0tn in 2025** and sector ROE fell from **17.5% to 7.78%**. The user-provided Bloomberg workbook confirms the investment trade-off: at 2025Q4, the audit-cleaned sample of ten banks had median common ROE of **9.68%**, P/B of **0.67x** and trailing P/E of **7.35x**, versus **14.78%**, **3.34x** and **17.29x** for a heterogeneous nine-name platform/fintech sample; by H1 2026, however, loan facilitators’ earnings had deteriorated sharply. The durable 2026 equilibrium is regulated co-opetition, with the strongest economics accruing to institutions that combine interface control with a licence, funding access or a defensible regulated partnership.

## A layered framework produces defensible shares

### Scope, classification and evidence rules

“Conventional banks” here means Mainland licensed banking institutions, with the major state banks and nationwide joint-stock banks used as listed comparables. **WeBank and MYbank are classified as licensed digital banks, not non-bank fintechs**, because they take deposits, maintain regulatory capital and hold loans on balance sheet. “Fintech” is subdivided into payment/distribution platforms such as Ant and Tencent; digital brokers and fund distributors such as East Money and Futu; non-bank credit facilitators such as Qfin, FinVolution, Lexin and Lufax; and broad platform or insurance groups such as Alibaba, JD and Ping An. These models differ so much in funding, geography, accounting and risk ownership that a single fintech total is analytically weak.

The evidence is tagged by status. **Actual** means a company filing or regulator observation for a completed period; **2026 H1/Q2 actual** is interim and is never annualised or presented as full-year 2026; **cached Bloomberg historical observation** means a field sampled by the user’s `BDH` workbook as of a quarter-end observation date; **point-in-time market observation** means a valuation on that date; **BI calculation** means Bloomberg Intelligence derived the figure, for example from Alibaba’s equity-accounting disclosure; **consensus** means the BI report labels a group forecast consensus but does not disclose contributor count; and **BI scenario/estimate** means an analyst model rather than consensus. The 2019 and 2021 BI `2025E` figures are preserved only as historical expectations. They are **not current forecasts and not realised 2025 actuals** ([BI `4.pdf`, p. 1](</Users/agatbe/Downloads/chinafintech/4.pdf>); [BI `5.pdf`, pp. 1–5](</Users/agatbe/Downloads/chinafintech/5.pdf>)).

### Five layers answer five different market-share questions

The PBOC defines bank electronic payments as account-changing transactions initiated through online, mobile, ATM, POS and other channels, while non-bank network payments include payment-institution transactions involving bank accounts as well as payment-account transactions. The overlap is explicit: adding the two does not create a unique population of purchases ([PBOC 2023 payment-system report, footnotes 11–13](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/zhifujiesuansi/128525/128545/128643/e65053157ab14b9abf06bc9b18badb3a/2024032815235914509.pdf)).

The mechanical activity index is nevertheless informative when it is labelled correctly. Bank electronic payments accounted for **62.7% of the two-series count and 99.1% of value in 2013**, compared with **19.4% and 91.5% in 2025**. The average bank-electronic ticket fell from about RMB41,700 to RMB11,332, while the non-bank average fell from roughly RMB601 to RMB255. Fintech therefore captured frequency much faster than high-value money movement ([PBOC 2013](https://www.gov.cn/gzdt/att/att/site1/20140217/782bcb8883ce146be61101.pdf); [2025 figures reproduced by Cuiwei](https://static.cninfo.com.cn/finalpage/2026-03-28/1225042491.PDF)).

### Customer use is multi-homed, not sector-exclusive

The typical client does not choose “a bank or a fintech.” The customer uses a fintech interface for speed and context, while the product, funding, custody or risk often remains with a licensed institution. Early evidence already showed multi-homing: in 2012, **79.2%** of surveyed online-payment users used third-party account balances and **75.7%** used online banking ([CNNIC](https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2022/0401/c119-1026.html)).

## Regulation made the two sectors interdependent

China’s regulatory trajectory moved from “licence and experiment” toward “same activity, same risk, same regulation.” The pivotal change was not the elimination of platforms but the removal of regulatory arbitrage: direct bilateral clearing and float income were curtailed, P2P disappeared, lending capital and approval returned toward licensed entities, and online distributors had to make the product provider and total borrower cost visible.

| Year | Rule or event | Structural effect |
|---:|---|---|
| 2010–2011 | PBOC payment licensing formalised Alipay, Tenpay and peers ([PBOC](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/english/130733/2025080815071811497/index.html)) | Legitimated scale inside a regulatory perimeter |
| 2015–2016 | Online-payment measures and internet-finance rectification required cross-bank payments through licensed clearing ([PBOC](https://zhengzhou.pbc.gov.cn/zhengzhou/124251/3167821/3169681/index.html)) | Began ending opaque direct platform-bank connections and P2P-style intermediation |
| 2017–2019 | QR rules, UnionPay/NetsUnion routing and 100% centralisation of customer reserves at the PBOC ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/publ/arpdf/ar2019e.pdf); [PBOC](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/chubanwu/114566/114579/4356045/4356177/2021100915270894643.pdf)) | Removed float income and made shared infrastructure explicit |
| 2018 | Asset-management rules attacked guaranteed returns and maturity mismatch; MMF instant redemption tightened ([PBOC](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/jinrongwendingju/146766/146770/c2e228ad641d4a85acdc9142da4f955d/index.html)) | Converged platform and bank wealth products toward regulated NAV structures |
| 2020 | Bank internet-loan and financial-holding rules tightened; Ant’s IPO was suspended; all operating P2P platforms closed ([HKEX](https://www1.hkexnews.hk/listedco/listconews/sehk/2020/1103/2020110302285.pdf); [BIS](https://www.bis.org/speeches/20201222-fintech-developments-challenges-supervision-china)) | Repriced thin-capital “technology” models and shifted credit into licensed structures |
| 2021 | Co-lenders required at least 30% contribution; bank partner/exposure limits imposed; Ant payment/credit separation and data rectification required ([CBIRC/MOFCOM](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zcfb/zgdwjjmywg/art/2021/art_b3faa0277ac64fefa328864628d4292c.html); [Ant rectification](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1577552/000110465921049222/a21-12722_1ex99d1.htm)) | Raised platform capital needs and banks’ partner bargaining power |
| 2021–2024 | QR interoperability broadened among Alipay, WeChat Pay and UnionPay/QuickPass ([Beijing government](https://www.beijing.gov.cn/fuwu/bmfw/sy/jrts/202112/t20211206_2553887.html); [CNNIC](https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2025/0117/c88-11229.html)) | Reduced exclusive acceptance as a moat; shifted competition toward engagement and adjacent services |
| 2023–2024 | Ant regulatory fine and new non-bank payment regulation effective May 2024 ([CSRC/PBOC](https://www.csrc.gov.cn/csrc/c100028/c7418599/content.shtml); [State Council Decree 768](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/tiaofasi/144941/144953/5174993/index.html)) | Ended concentrated rectification but institutionalised capital, AML, data and user-protection costs |
| 2025 | Internet loan-assistance rules effective 1 October required head-office whitelists, all-in cost disclosure and tighter fee/revenue-sharing controls ([State Council/NFRA](https://app.www.gov.cn/govdata/gov/202504/04/526105/article.html)) | Increased bank control over platform access and compressed loan-facilitator take rates |
| 2026 | Personal-loan cost disclosure effective 1 August and online financial-product marketing rules effective 30 September restricted third-party control of contracting, fund transfer, suitability and limits ([NFRA cost rules](https://www.nfra.gov.cn/en/view/pages/ItemDetail.html?docId=1252542&itemId=981); [NFRA marketing rules](https://www.nfra.gov.cn/en/view/pages/ItemDetail.html?docId=1257542&itemId=981)) | Preserved platforms as marketing/service layers while making licensed institutions visibly responsible |

This structure makes each sector both competitor and supplier to the other. Platforms reduce acquisition and servicing costs, generate transaction data, reach thin-file borrowers and pressure banks to improve UX. Banks provide deposit funding, KYC, cards, licences, underwriting, custody and settlement. In wealth, Ant Fund and Tiantian compete with CMB for distribution while selling products made and custodied by regulated institutions. In credit, the relationship is even clearer: `4.pdf` counted **2,000 Ant funding partners, 600 at JD Tech, 112 at Qudian, 100 at Lexin, 91 at 360 DigiTech, 50 at Lufax and 30 at WeBank**. These counts show network breadth, not committed funding or outstanding exposure ([BI `4.pdf`, p. 2](</Users/agatbe/Downloads/chinafintech/4.pdf>)).

The 2026 loan-facilitator downturn exposes where bargaining power now sits. FinVolution’s Mainland Q2 volume fell 19.3% YoY, Qfin’s fell 25.1%, and firms cited tighter institutional funding or regulation after the 2025 rules ([FinVolution](https://ir.finvgroup.com/news-releases/news-release-details/finvolution-group-reports-second-quarter-2026-unaudited); [Qfin](https://360finance.gcs-web.com/news-releases/news-release-details/qfin-holdings-announces-second-quarter-and-interim-2026)). App traffic could not insulate earnings when bank partners tightened funding and economics.

## Investment conclusions favour licensed hybrids

The best long-term positions combine at least two of three advantages: customer context, regulated balance-sheet/product ownership and durable funding/partner access. **CMB** combines deposit funding with mobile and wealth engagement; **WeBank and MYbank** turn ecosystems into licensed deposit-and-loan franchises; **East Money** converts information traffic into regulated brokerage and distribution; **Ant and Tencent** retain exceptional interface optionality but weaker segment transparency; and the large state banks retain funding scale and policy relevance but face thin margins and slower profit growth. Pure loan-referral models have the weakest bargaining position because banks now control whitelists, funding, approval and total borrower cost.

The valuation evidence supports a barbell, not a blanket sector call. Banks’ 0.67x median book value and 4.20% dividend yield at 2025Q4 compensate for slow profit growth, policy pricing and macro-credit exposure, but cheapness alone does not reverse NIM compression. Fintech’s higher 3.34x book and 14.78% common ROE reflect capital-light economics, but the H1 2026 profit collapse among facilitators and the July forward-P/E discounts show that a low multiple can price a damaged earnings denominator. Brokers and investment platforms benefited from market activity in 2025, whereas lenders remained exposed to funding, APR and provisioning. Ant Consumer Finance’s RMB303bn book provides the cleanest case study: balance-sheet growth was real, but credit costs nearly absorbed NII.

| Investment question | Bank evidence to monitor | Fintech/platform evidence to monitor | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does engagement monetise? | Mobile MAU, net fees, retail AUM, digital sales, cost/income | Transacting users, merchants, TPV, take rate, fund holdings | Registrations are weak; active, funded and transacting measures are superior |
| Who owns funding and risk? | Deposits, loan growth, NIM, CET1, credit cost, NPL, coverage | On-balance loans, guarantees, capital-light share, partner funding, DPD90 | Originations cannot be compared directly with bank assets |
| Is regulation shifting bargaining power? | Partner whitelist, internal digital origination, funding cost | Partner concentration, approval rate, all-in APR and fee share | 2025–2026 rules favour licensed institutions and transparent economics |
| Is wealth a durable fee pool? | WMP/fund/insurance AUM, custody, private-bank clients | Distributed holdings, sales, active investors, trail-fee revenue | The distributor can own shelf space without owning the assets |
| Is growth profitable? | Profit versus asset growth, ROE, ROA, NIM | Revenue/profit per active user, CAC, credit losses, capital consumed | Scale without returns is not value; volume without take rate/risk data is not value |

The major bank risks are policy-driven margin pressure, property/local-government/SME credit costs and deposit competition. Fintech risks are funding-partner concentration, fee or APR caps, consumer-protection/data constraints, opaque private-company disclosure, cyber/fraud risk and capital-market cyclicality. Interoperability and e-CNY can commoditise closed acceptance, while Alipay/WeChat concentration invites continued antitrust scrutiny. The BI downside idea of a 12% all-in lending-rate threshold by end-2027 is only a **scenario**, not enacted regulation, and is not included in the regulatory chronology ([BI `1.pdf`, pp. 2–3](</Users/agatbe/Downloads/chinafintech/1.pdf>)).

## Conclusion

China’s finance stack now has a clear division of labour. Fintech controls much of the high-frequency interface and product discovery; banks and other licensed institutions control the liability, the licence, much of the credit risk, the custodian account and final settlement. This is why non-banks can dominate transaction count, Alipay and WeChat can dominate wallet initiation, and conventional banks can simultaneously grow assets and regain regulatory bargaining power.

The investment edge comes from refusing category shortcuts. A payment share is not a deposit share, an enabled loan is not an owned asset, AUM distributed is not AUM manufactured, and a registered user is not an active customer. The strongest franchises are those that can turn interface engagement into transparent recurring revenue while maintaining durable access to licensed funding, product manufacture and capital; the weakest are those whose volume disappears when a bank partner, risk rule or all-in price changes.
