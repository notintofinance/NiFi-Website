# China’s fintech interface rides bank rails

**Bottom line.** Mainland China does not have one defensible “conventional-bank versus fintech transaction share,” because the same payment can be initiated in Alipay, funded from a bank deposit, routed through NetsUnion or UnionPay, and settled on bank/PBOC infrastructure. The best answer is layered. On a deliberately mechanical, overlapping activity index, non-bank payment institutions represented **80.6% of the combined bank-electronic/non-bank transaction count but only 8.5% of value in 2025**; banks represented **19.4% of count and 91.5% of value**. In **2026 H1 actual data**, the equivalent count split was **80.0% non-bank/20.0% bank**, while the value split was **7.6%/92.4%**. At the consumer-wallet front end, Alipay and Tenpay/WeChat Pay together held roughly **90–95% of third-party mobile-payment activity from the late 2010s through 2026**, but that is not 90–95% of all Chinese money movement. Fintech therefore won frequency, discovery and everyday engagement; banks retained deposits, long-duration funding, most credit risk, settlement, custody and large-ticket corporate finance. From 2010 to 2025, banking assets still quintupled from **RMB95.3tn to RMB480.0tn**, even as fintech payment usage became near-universal; the cost was weaker profitability, with sector ROE falling from **17.5% to 7.78%**. The stable 2026 equilibrium is regulated co-opetition: platforms acquire and serve customers, while licensed banks and product manufacturers fund, underwrite, settle and custody much of what the platforms distribute.

## A layered denominator replaces a false market share

### Methodology and definitions

“Conventional banks” in this report means Mainland licensed banking institutions, with ICBC, CCB, ABC, BOC and CMB as representative listed banks. WeBank and MYbank are classified separately as **digital banks**, not as non-bank fintechs, because they hold banking licences, deposits, capital and on-balance-sheet loans. “Fintech” means non-bank payment platforms, technology/distribution ecosystems, digital brokers and loan facilitators; these businesses should not be aggregated as if they shared one funding model. All 2010–2025 figures are historical actuals unless marked as a market-research estimate. **2026 figures are H1/Q2 or latest YTD actuals, not full-year actuals**; no 2026 full-year forecast is inserted into the historical series. Alipay/WeChat brand shares are third-party estimates because the PBOC does not publish provider-level shares.

The unit of analysis must match the economic question. A wallet “owns” the customer interface, but the funding instrument can be a bank deposit or card; a merchant acquirer or clearing network can process the transaction; and a commercial bank or the PBOC ultimately provides the settlement asset. The PBOC explicitly defines non-bank network payments to include payment-institution transactions involving bank accounts, while bank electronic payments cover account-changing transactions through online, mobile, ATM, POS and other channels. Adding the two series therefore double counts some economic payments ([PBOC 2023 payment-system report, footnotes 11–13](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/zhifujiesuansi/128525/128545/128643/e65053157ab14b9abf06bc9b18badb3a/2024032815235914509.pdf)).

The closest numerical “share” answer is thus the activity index, not an ownership claim. Its changing shape is still informative: bank electronic payments accounted for **62.7% of combined count and 99.1% of combined value in 2013**, versus **19.4% and 91.5% in 2025**. Non-banks captured small-ticket frequency far faster than high-value money movement. Average ticket size reinforces the distinction: the implied bank-electronic average fell from about **RMB41,700 in 2013 to RMB11,332 in 2025**, while the non-bank average fell from about **RMB601 to RMB255** ([PBOC 2013](https://www.gov.cn/gzdt/att/att/site1/20140217/782bcb8883ce146be61101.pdf); [2025 figures reproduced by Cuiwei](https://static.cninfo.com.cn/finalpage/2026-03-28/1225042491.PDF)). These are heterogeneous channel averages, but they capture the strategic split: fintech handles many low-ticket moments; banks handle fewer but much larger value transfers.

The consumer-interface market consolidated just as quickly. Alipay and Tenpay together held an estimated **72.0% of third-party payments in 2010** and **67.2% of third-party internet-payment value in 2011**. In mobile payment, their combined share reached **91% in 2016 Q4**, **93.86% in 2020 Q2**, roughly **94% in 2024**, and close to **95% of volume in the IMF’s 2026 assessment** ([2010 market summary](https://www.marbridgeconsulting.com/marbridgedaily/2011-01-21/article/43020/china_2010_e_commerce_market_reaches_rmb_45_trln); [Analysys 2011 via the Internet Society of China](https://www.isc.org.cn/article/19006.html); [iResearch 2016 chart](https://e-research.siam.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/IMBA-2018-IS-The-Analysis-of-Critical-Factors-for-Mobile-Payment-Competition-in-China-Evidence-from-Alipay_compressed.pdf); [Caixin/Analysys 2020](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2021-02-08/four-things-to-know-about-threats-to-chinas-payment-giants-101662049.html); [AMRO 2025](https://amro-asia.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/AMRO-AFSR-2025-Full-Report-Web.pdf); [IMF 2026](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/005/2026/009/article-A001-en.xml)). The denominator changed from online payments to mobile wallets, so this is a directional provider-share history, not one continuous audited series.

## Fintech won frequency while banks kept value

The PBOC activity series shows two simultaneous digital transformations rather than bank stagnation and fintech replacement. Bank mobile-payment volume grew from **118m transactions in 2010 to 231.5bn in 2025**, a calculated **65.8% CAGR**, while value rose from **RMB0.57tn to RMB571.97tn**, a **58.5% CAGR** ([PBOC 2010](https://www.gov.cn/gzdt/att/att/site1/20110720/1c6f6506c2380f90ec390e.pdf); [2025 figures reproduced by Cuiwei](https://static.cninfo.com.cn/finalpage/2026-03-28/1225042491.PDF)). On the more comparable 2013–2025 broad series, bank electronic-payment count grew at **23.3% CAGR** and value at **10.7%**, while non-bank network-payment count grew at **45.0%** and value at **35.0%**, subject to major classification breaks in 2014, 2015, 2018 and 2023.

## Bank balance sheets quintupled as returns halved

The banking system remained the dominant funded intermediary. Assets expanded from **RMB95.3tn in 2010 to RMB480.0tn in 2025**, deposits from **RMB73.3tn to RMB336.1tn**, loans from **RMB50.9tn to RMB275.7tn**, and net profit from roughly **RMB899bn to RMB2.4tn**. Calculated 2010–2025 CAGRs were **11.4% for assets, 10.7% for deposits, 11.9% for loans and only 6.8% for profit**. The deceleration is material: asset CAGR slowed from **15.9% in 2010–2015** to **8.5% in 2020–2025**, while ROE fell from **17.5% to 7.78%** and ROA from **1.03% to 0.60%** ([CBRC 2010 annual report](https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/subject/subject/nianbao2010/english/1.pdf); [NFRA 2025 indicators](https://www.nfra.gov.cn/en/view/pages/ItemDetail.html?docId=1251267&itemId=980)). Fintech growth therefore coincided with a much larger banking system, but that system earned less per unit of balance sheet.

The most useful apples-to-apples comparison is not fintech TPV against bank assets. It is **interface productivity**—active users, transactions, TPV, fund sales and originations—alongside **economic ownership**—net revenue/take rate, on-balance-sheet loans, guarantees, provisions and capital. Ant’s 2019 revenue mix illustrates the logic: digital payments and merchant services contributed **RMB51.9bn, or 43.0%**, while CreditTech, InvestmentTech and InsureTech together contributed **RMB67.8bn, or 56.2%**. ## Clients combine wallets, banks and specialists

The typical user does not choose one sector. The dominant journey is **transaction first, finance second**: a consumer scans an Alipay or WeChat code, but the wallet may debit a bank deposit or card; the same app then offers a money-market fund, consumer credit or insurance manufactured by a licensed partner. At the same time, salary, mortgage, corporate treasury and large wealth balances remain anchored at banks. Multi-homing was visible early: in 2012, **79.2%** of surveyed online-payment users used third-party account balances and **75.7%** used online banking ([CNNIC 2012 payment-safety report](https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2022/0401/c119-1026.html)). By 2023, a consumer survey found roughly nine in ten Mainland online-payment users had used **both** Alipay and WeChat Pay in the prior year, while 45% had used UnionPay; those are overlapping penetration rates, not transaction shares ([Statista](https://www.statista.com/chart/17409/most-popular-digital-payment-services-in-china/)).

### User-perspective journey and use-case map

| Customer job | Usual front door | Where funds/product/risk sit | Why both sectors persist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily merchant payment, P2P, red packet, bills | Alipay, WeChat Pay, bank app/card, UnionPay QuickPass | Commonly a bank account/card or regulated payment balance; cleared through UnionPay/NetsUnion and settled via banks/PBOC | Wallets optimise convenience and social/commerce context; banks provide accounts, cards, KYC and liquidity. From 2018, bank-linked wallet traffic moved to licensed clearing ([BIS 2019](https://www.bis.org/publ/arpdf/ar2019e.pdf)) |

| Spare cash and mutual funds | Alipay/Yu’e Bao/Ant Fortune, Licaitong, Tiantian, CMB/other bank apps | Fund manager or bank wealth subsidiary manufactures assets; bank/custodian holds them | Platforms win discovery, low minimums and comparison; banks and asset managers retain regulated manufacture/custody |
| Wealth management and advice | Bank app/relationship manager for affluent users; Ant/Tiantian for self-directed users | Bank WMP subsidiary, fund manager, insurer or custodian | Bank WMP balances reached **RMB29.95tn with 125m investors in 2024** despite platform growth ([State Council/Xinhua](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202501/17/content_WS678a376cc6d0868f4e8eee9d.html)) |

Futu’s global data are not a Mainland market-share proxy |

Risk-bearing mix determines economics |
| Mortgage and longer consumer finance | Bank branch/app | Bank balance sheet, collateral servicing and capital | At end-2025, medium/long-term consumer loans were **RMB48.7tn**, far beyond platform loan books ([NBS](https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/PressRelease/202602/t20260228_1962661.html)) |
| Micro/SME borrowing | MYbank/WeBank/commerce platforms plus ABC and other bank channels | Digital bank or partner bank balance sheet; sometimes guarantee/facilitation layer | Platform data improve thin-file underwriting; banks supply deposits, capital and policy-linked scale. | Corporate working capital, project/trade finance, cash management | Relationship bank and corporate digital banking | Bank balance sheet, guarantees, FX and payment infrastructure | Tickets, covenants, tenor and cross-border networks favour banks. BOC handled roughly **25% of China’s cross-border settlement in 2025** ([BOC](https://wap.boc.cn/bif/bi1/202603/t20260330_25657386.html)) |
| Insurance | Alipay/WeSure/bank app/agent | Licensed insurer bears risk; bank or platform distributes | Front ends compete for commission and data while insurers retain underwriting capital |
| e-CNY | Bank app, e-CNY app, Alipay or WeChat interface | Central-bank money; from 2026, real-name commercial-bank wallet balances are described as interest-bearing commercial-bank liabilities covered by deposit insurance | e-CNY can strengthen public/bank settlement without displacing familiar fintech interfaces ([IMF 2025](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/063/2025/007/article-A001-en.xml); [MOFCOM/CNS](https://dzswgf.mofcom.gov.cn/news/43/2025/12/1767072924980.html)) |

Wealth distribution is the clearest area of direct channel substitution. At June 2020, Ant reported **RMB4.099tn of InvestmentTech AUM**, but this represented products enabled or distributed from around 170 asset managers rather than Ant-owned assets ([Ant prospectus](https://wynthia.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/ant-draft-ipo-prospectus-2020-08-read.pdf)). In H1 2025, Ant Fund held **RMB1.568tn of non-money-market mutual funds**, CMB **RMB1.042tn**, and Tiantian **RMB637.4bn**; the ranking directly mixes third-party platforms and banks in one regulated distribution market ([AMAC data reported by Tiger Brokers](https://www.itiger.com/news/1152389806); [AMAC portal](https://www.amac.org.cn/sjtj/datastatistics/fundsalesindustrydata/)). The platform can own the shelf and customer attention while a fund manager and bank custodian retain the assets.

Credit requires even stricter separation. Ant’s June-2020 **RMB1.732tn consumer and RMB422bn SME enabled balances** were an origination/distribution measure, not Ant’s funded book. The 2021 internet-loan rules forced joint-lending partners to contribute at least **30% of each loan** and capped partner concentration, moving capital responsibility back toward licensed institutions ([CBIRC/MOFCOM text](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zcfb/zgdwjjmywg/art/2021/art_b3faa0277ac64fefa328864628d4292c.html)). By Q2 2026, roughly half of Qfin’s and FinVolution’s outstanding balances were in capital-light or technology modes, whereas Lufax bore risk on **93.2%** of its outstanding book; equal originations therefore imply very different earnings volatility and capital intensity ([Qfin Q2 2026](https://360finance.gcs-web.com/news-releases/news-release-details/qfin-holdings-announces-second-quarter-and-interim-2026); [FinVolution Q2 2026](https://ir.finvgroup.com/news-releases/news-release-details/finvolution-group-reports-second-quarter-2026-unaudited); [Lufax Q2 2026](https://ir.lufaxholding.com/2026-08-18-Lufax-Reports-Second-Quarter-2026-Financial-Results)).

## Regulation converted disruption into co-opetition

China’s regulatory arc moved from licensing and experimentation to “same activity, same risk, same regulation.” The turning point was not a ban on fintech but removal of regulatory arbitrage: wallets lost opaque direct bank connections and float income; P2P disappeared; banks regained underwriting and partner-control responsibility; finance products had to identify the licensed provider; and data use became consent- and purpose-bound. This increased banks’ bargaining power without erasing the platforms’ distribution advantage.

| Year | Rule or event | Structural effect on banks and fintech |
|---:|---|---|
| 2010–2011 | PBOC payment-services licensing formalised Alipay, Tenpay and peers ([PBOC](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/english/130733/2025080815071811497/index.html)) | Legitimated non-bank payments and enabled mass scaling inside a regulatory perimeter |
| 2015–2016 | Online-payment rules and internet-finance rectification required cross-bank payments to use licensed clearing and began ending direct platform-bank connections ([PBOC](https://zhengzhou.pbc.gov.cn/zhengzhou/124251/3167821/3169681/index.html)) | Shifted platforms from quasi-clearing utilities toward licensed payment institutions; began P2P unwind |
| 2017–2019 | QR rules, mandatory UnionPay/NetsUnion routing and 100% centralisation of customer reserves at the PBOC ([BIS](https://www.bis.org/publ/arpdf/ar2019e.pdf); [PBOC notice](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/chubanwu/114566/114579/4356045/4356177/2021100915270894643.pdf)) | Eliminated much float income and bilateral routing opacity; made shared infrastructure explicit |
| 2018 | New asset-management rules attacked guaranteed returns and maturity mismatch; instant MMF redemption was restricted ([PBOC](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/jinrongwendingju/146766/146770/c2e228ad641d4a85acdc9142da4f955d/index.html)) | Slowed Yu’e Bao-style balance concentration and pushed platform/bank wealth products toward NAV-based regulated structures |
| 2020 | Bank internet-loan rules and financial-holding supervision tightened; Ant’s IPO was suspended on 3 November ([HKEX](https://www1.hkexnews.hk/listedco/listconews/sehk/2020/1103/2020110302285.pdf); [State Council](https://english.www.gov.cn/policies/latestreleases/202009/13/content_WS5f5e16e2c6d0f7257693be8f.html)) | Repriced “technology company” regulatory arbitrage and forced licensed capital, governance and bank-controlled risk |
| 2020 | All operating P2P platforms were closed by mid-November ([PBOC/CBIRC chairman](https://www.bis.org/speeches/20201222-fintech-developments-challenges-supervision-china)) | Retail-investor-funded shadow lending disappeared; licensed bank/consumer-finance funding replaced it |
| 2021 | Co-lenders required ≥30% contribution; bank exposure limits imposed; Ant required payment-credit separation, licensed credit reporting and stronger data protection ([CBIRC/MOFCOM](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zcfb/zgdwjjmywg/art/2021/art_b3faa0277ac64fefa328864628d4292c.html); [Ant rectification](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1577552/000110465921049222/a21-12722_1ex99d1.htm)) | Increased platform capital needs and national-bank partner value; weakened walled-garden data advantages |
| 2021–2024 | QR interoperability broadened among Alipay, WeChat Pay and UnionPay/QuickPass ([Beijing government](https://www.beijing.gov.cn/fuwu/bmfw/sy/jrts/202112/t20211206_2553887.html); [CNNIC](https://www3.cnnic.cn/n4/2025/0117/c88-11229.html)) | Reduced exclusive acceptance as a moat; shifted competition toward engagement, merchant software and adjacent finance |
| 2023–2024 | Ant paid a major regulatory fine; the new non-bank payment regulation took effect 1 May 2024 with capital, AML, data and user-protection requirements ([CSRC/PBOC](https://www.csrc.gov.cn/csrc/c100028/c7418599/content.shtml); [State Council Decree 768](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/tiaofasi/144941/144953/5174993/index.html)) | Ended the concentrated rectification phase but institutionalised stricter licensed-operation economics |
| 2025 | Internet loan-assistance rules effective 1 October required head-office whitelists, all-in cost disclosure and tighter platform-fee/revenue-sharing controls ([State Council/NFRA](https://app.www.gov.cn/govdata/gov/202504/04/526105/article.html)) | Increased bank partner-selection power and compressed lender-platform take rates |
| 2026 | All-in personal-loan cost disclosure effective 1 August and online financial-product marketing rules effective 30 September bar third-party platforms from controlling contracting, fund transfer, suitability and credit-limit assessment ([NFRA cost rules](https://www.nfra.gov.cn/en/view/pages/ItemDetail.html?docId=1252542&itemId=981); [NFRA marketing rules](https://www.nfra.gov.cn/en/view/pages/ItemDetail.html?docId=1257542&itemId=981)) | Preserved platforms as marketing/service interfaces while making licensed institutions visibly responsible for pricing, suitability and approval |

The operating model now shares economics across layers. A wallet can earn merchant or technology fees and use payment data to distribute credit or funds; the acquirer and clearing network earn processing fees; the issuing bank retains the deposit or card relationship and provides settlement liquidity; a licensed bank, insurer or asset manager manufactures and bears the financial risk. This is why fintech both pressures and grows banks. It compresses simple card, payment and branch-distribution margins, but it also creates more digital transactions, reaches thin-file borrowers, lowers servicing costs and supplies origination to banks with deposits and capital. Banks in turn make fintech scalable by providing the regulated products, funding, custody and settlement that platform customers consume.

The 2026 online-lender contraction shows where dependence sits. FinVolution’s Mainland Q2-2026 volume fell **19.3% YoY**, Qfin’s fell **25.1%**, and several platforms cited tighter institutional funding or regulatory pressure after the 2025 loan-assistance rules ([FinVolution](https://ir.finvgroup.com/news-releases/news-release-details/finvolution-group-reports-second-quarter-2026-unaudited); [Qfin](https://360finance.gcs-web.com/news-releases/news-release-details/qfin-holdings-announces-second-quarter-and-interim-2026); [Lexin](https://lexinfintech.gcs-web.com/news-releases/news-release-details/lexinfintech-holdings-ltd-reports-second-quarter-2026-unaudited)). App traffic did not protect the model when funding partners pulled back. Conversely, bank apps achieved hundreds of millions of MAU partly because fintech reset expectations for instant, low-cost, personalised service.

e-CNY reinforces rather than dissolves the layered model. Cumulative trial activity reached **RMB9.4tn and 2.56bn transactions by end-2024**, then **RMB14.2tn and 3.32bn transactions by end-September 2025**, with 225m personal app wallets. These are cumulative pilot totals, not annual retail TPV ([PBOC](https://www.pbc.gov.cn/xindaishichangsi/5443861/5443865/5443892/5957f8d15a824e9e88d01710873482d3/2025110711361912922.pdf); [State Council/Xinhua](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202510/29/content_WS6901a9c9c6d00ca5f9a0726a.html)). Because e-CNY can appear inside bank, Alipay and WeChat interfaces, it may increase public settlement visibility while leaving customer-interface competition largely intact.

## Investment returns favour licensed, data-rich hybrids

The central investment conclusion is not “buy banks” or “buy fintech.” It is to identify who controls a high-frequency interface, who owns the regulated liability or asset, and how the fee is shared. The strongest models combine at least two of those advantages. CMB pairs a bank deposit franchise with unusually strong mobile and wealth engagement; WeBank and MYbank combine ecosystem distribution with licensed bank balance sheets; East Money converts information/community traffic into regulated brokerage and fund distribution; Tencent and Ant retain exceptional payment/distribution reach but have limited segment transparency; large state banks retain dominant funding and policy relevance but face thin margins and slower profit growth. Pure traffic or loan-referral platforms have the weakest position because regulation now gives banks control over whitelists, approval, total borrower cost and funding.

| Investable question | Bank indicators | Fintech/platform indicators | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Is customer engagement monetising? | Mobile MAU, digital sales share, net fee income, retail AUM, cards/spend, cost-to-income | Transacting users, merchants, TPV, take rate, fund holdings, cross-sell users | Registered accounts are weak evidence; active/funded/transacting measures are superior |
| Who owns funding and risk? | Deposits, loan growth, NIM, CET1, credit cost, NPL, provision coverage | On-balance loans, guarantee exposure, capital-light share, partner funding, DPD90 | Platform originations should never be compared directly with bank assets |
| Is regulation shifting bargaining power? | Partner whitelist, funding cost, platform-fee share, internal digital origination | Partner concentration, approval rate, all-in APR, revenue share, marketing restrictions | 2025–2026 rules favour well-capitalised licensed partners and transparent economics |
| Is wealth a durable fee pool? | WMP/fund/insurance AUM, advisory penetration, custody, private-bank clients | Distributed fund holdings, sales, active investors, trail-fee revenue | Platforms can own shelf space while banks/asset managers retain manufacture and custody |
| Is growth profitable? | Profit growth versus asset growth, ROE/ROA/NIM | Revenue and profit per active user, credit losses, CAC, capital intensity | Banks’ scale growth is no longer enough; fintech volume is meaningless without take rate and risk cost |

The clearest bank risk is macro-credit and margin pressure, not wallet extinction. Sector NIM fell to **1.42% in 2025**, while property, local-government and SME exposures can raise credit costs even if headline NPL ratios remain stable. Large banks can continue gaining share but still deliver modest returns if policy pricing and deposit competition compress spreads. Fintech’s principal risks are regulatory fee caps, funding-partner concentration, data/privacy constraints, rising required capital, opaque private-company disclosure and the cyclicality of wealth/brokerage activity. Both sectors face cyber, fraud and operational resilience risk; interoperability and e-CNY can commoditise closed payment acceptance; concentration in Alipay/WeChat also attracts continued antitrust and consumer-protection scrutiny.

## Conclusion

China’s financial industry did not move from banks to fintech; it reorganised into a stack. Fintech captured the first click, the QR code and much of daily behavioural data, while banks retained the deposit, licence, settlement account, balance sheet and much of the risk. That division explains the apparently contradictory facts that non-banks now dominate transaction frequency, the Alipay–WeChat duopoly dominates wallet initiation, and large banks simultaneously gained asset share.

The investment edge comes from measuring the right layer. A high TPV or origination number is valuable only if it converts into durable net revenue without hidden capital or credit exposure; a huge bank balance sheet is valuable only if deposits, digital engagement and fee franchises defend returns against NIM compression. The strongest long-run positions are therefore licensed, data-rich hybrids or partnerships—institutions that control customer context while retaining transparent access to funding, product manufacture and regulatory capital.
