Banks vs fintech in China, 2010–2026: fintech won the click, banks kept the balance sheet

Over 16 years, fintech platforms took about 80% of China's electronic payment transactions, but banks still move about 92% of the value, hold almost all deposits and fund most credit. The two sectors did not replace each other. They split into layers of one stack, and since 2020 regulation has moved bargaining power back toward the licensed balance sheet.

Q1 · Market share: how much goes to banks and how much to fintech?

The answer depends on the layer. Fintech has 80.0% of transaction count but only 7.6% of value (2026 H1). Alipay and WeChat Pay control ~95% of retail mobile wallets. Third-party platforms overtook banks in mutual-fund distribution for the first time in H1 2026 (39.2% vs 38.7%). Banks still hold ~99.7% of system loans and deposits. WeBank and MYbank, the platform-born licensed banks, have about 0.3% of loans. Ant alone enabled about 21% of short-term consumer credit at its 2020 peak, and 98% of that was funded by banks.

Q2 · Growth: who grew faster?

Fintech grew faster in activity. Non-bank payment value compounded at 35.0% a year (2013–2025), against 10.7% for all bank e-payments. Banks grew faster in scale: assets went from RMB95tn to RMB498tn. But bank profitability halved, with sector ROE falling from 17.5% to 7.7% and median NIM from 2.83% to 1.38%. Platform economics peaked in 2019–2021 and then split: wealth and brokerage platforms are growing, while loan facilitators are shrinking (2026E consensus net income −21%).

Q3 · Usage: what do clients use each for?

Clients use fintech for frequent, small, contextual jobs: 77.6% of mobile-payment users mostly pay under RMB100, plus small-ticket credit and self-directed funds. They use banks for large, sticky, trust-heavy jobs: salary, deposits (RMB167tn household), mortgages, affluent wealth and corporate finance. The user does not choose one or the other: 80.9% of wallet payers fund payments from a bank debit card and 69.3% from a credit card.

1.1 Payments: fintech wins count, banks keep value

What
The non-bank share of combined electronic payment count rose from 37.3% in 2013 to 80.0% in 2026 H1. Its share of value peaked at 10.7% in 2021 and slipped back to 7.6%.
Why
QR codes made small payments nearly free and social (red packets, P2P). Large transfers such as payroll, B2B, property and securities settlement still run bank-to-bank. The 2021–2025 value decline reflects the platform rectification, weaker consumption and the 2023 statistical change that removed double-counted inter-provider flows.
Impact
Average non-bank tickets fell from about RMB601 to RMB255, while bank tickets are about RMB11,000. Platforms own behavioural data and frequency. Banks own liquidity and settlement value, and the value layer is where interest margin and float income sit.

Question 2

Growth: fintech compounded activity, banks compounded assets but lost returns

What
Bank mobile-payment value compounded at 58.5% a year (2010–2025) and non-bank network payments at 35.0% (2013–2025). Banking assets compounded at 11.4%, but sector profit grew only 6.8% a year and ROE fell from 17.5% to 7.7%.
Why
Fintech did not stop banks digitising; it forced them to. Bank margins were compressed for three reasons: interest-rate liberalisation (2013–2015, partly triggered by Yu'e Bao), policy-directed lending to SMEs, property and LGFVs, and repeated LPR/deposit-rate cuts. Platform growth slowed in 2020–2021, when regulators removed float income, P2P and thin-capital lending.
Impact
Banks are priced as low-growth utilities (median P/B from 1.6x to 0.6x, 4.2% yield). Platforms trade at a premium (2–3x book), but the premium now depends on the sub-model: brokers and wealth platforms are re-rating, while lenders trade at 1.8–3.6x forward P/E.

Question 3

Client usage: same customer, different jobs

What
In 2025, 88.5% of mobile-payment users paid by phone every day, 93.1% used QR codes, and 77.6% usually paid under RMB100. The money behind those payments was mostly bank money: 80.9% fund from debit cards, 69.3% from credit cards and only 41.2% from wallet balances (PCAC 2025 survey).
Why
Wallets solve context: merchants, social, e-commerce and super-app services. Banks solve trust and size: payroll, deposit insurance, mortgages and RM advice. Regulation reinforces the split: platforms cannot take deposits, and since 2021 they cannot sell bank deposits online.
Impact
Platform revenue comes from fees on others' balance sheets. Ant's H1 2020 mix was CreditTech 39.4%, payments 35.9%, InvestmentTech 15.6% and InsureTech 8.4%. Bank revenue is still mostly spread: fees are 20–30% of revenue at traditional banks against 44% at WeBank in 2018 (BI). Fintech monetises flow; banks monetise stock.

How they coexist, share, hurt and grow each other

The 16 years fall into four phases. Each started with a regulatory or product shock that changed which side held bargaining power.

2010–2012 · LICENSING

Fintech gets a legal perimeter

PBOC payment licences (2011) legitimise Alipay and Tenpay. Online-payment value is only about RMB1–7tn. Banks still have 99% of value and ROE above 20%. The two sectors barely interact.

2013–2017 · DISRUPTION

Platforms attack deposits, cards and credit

Yu'e Bao (June 2013) reaches RMB574bn in a year and RMB1.69tn by March 2018, pulling deposits into money funds and accelerating deposit-rate liberalisation (2015). WeChat red packets (2014) make Tenpay a payment rival. P2P peaks at about 5,000 platforms and RMB2.8tn of annual volume in 2017. Bank ROE falls from 21% to 14%.

2017–2020 · RECONNECTION

Regulators route platforms back through bank rails

NetsUnion (2018) ends direct platform–bank links. 100% of customer reserves are centralised at the PBOC (Jan 2019), which removes float income. Asset-management rules (2018) curb Yu'e Bao. Banks partner instead: about 98% of Ant's enabled credit is funded by partner banks or securitisation, and the WeBank/MYbank model scales.

2020–2023 · RECTIFICATION

The licence beats the interface

Ant's IPO is halted (Nov 2020) and P2P goes to zero. Co-lenders must fund at least 30% (2021), and online deposit sales are banned. Ant becomes a financial holding company and receives a RMB7.1bn fine (2023). The non-bank share of payment value peaks at 10.7% and turns down.

2024–2026 · REGULATED CO-OPETITION

Banks control funding; platforms control distribution

New payment regulation (May 2024) and internet loan-assistance whitelists (Oct 2025) require all-in cost disclosure (Aug 2026) and give licensed lenders control of contracting (Sep 2026). Facilitator volumes fall about 20–25%. In wealth, however, platforms overtake banks in fund distribution as RMB30–60tn of time deposits mature into a world of sub-1% deposit rates.

5.1 Positioning: a barbell, not a sector call

Income leg: large-cap Chinese banks. Valuations are 0.6x P/B and about 6.5x P/E with a 4.2% yield. Earnings are low-growth but policy-anchored, and the state's interest in stable dividends supports the payout. Prefer banks that own wealth relationships, since deposit migration is a fee opportunity for them rather than a pure loss. The risks are NIM below 1.3%, property and LGFV credit, and SME policy lending.

Growth leg: platforms that distribute without taking credit risk. Wealth and brokerage platforms (median 2025 revenue growth 53%, ROE 24% for East Money/Futu) and diversified ecosystems with fintech segments (Tencent FBS, 2026E +10%). The risks are cross-border brokerage enforcement, fee caps and market-turnover cyclicality.

Underweight: pure loan facilitators (Qfin, FinVolution, Lexin), until take rates stabilise. Their 1.8–3.6x forward P/E prices a shrinking earnings base, not a bargain, and banks now set whitelists, funding cost and all-in APR.

This is sector analysis for research purposes, not a personalised investment recommendation. Multiples and estimates are point-in-time (workbook data to 30 Jun 2026; BI views dated Jul–Oct 2026).

5.2 What would change the view

  • A sustained rebound in household credit demand would re-rate lenders and recover bank NIM.
  • Regulators lifting the platform deposit-product ban or allowing platform-owned risk-bearing would shift the balance back toward fintech.
  • e-CNY gaining merchant share at the expense of wallets: it is at 10.8% usage in 2025, with interest-bearing bank-wallet balances from 2026.
  • An Ant (or Ant International) IPO, which would give the first market price for the leading platform since 2020.

D. Method and caveats

  • Overlap. PBOC bank e-payments and non-bank network payments overlap, because a wallet payment funded by a bank card can appear in both. The share index measures activity, not exclusive ownership.
  • Statistical breaks in 2014, 2015, 2018 and 2023 affect year-on-year comparisons. 2010–2012 non-bank values are consultancy estimates.
  • Classification. WeBank and MYbank are licensed banks born from platforms. They are shown as "platform-side" in share tables but carry bank economics.
  • Workbook. 601995 CH (CICC) was excluded from banks and the duplicate JD removed. Q3-2026 fundamentals in the workbook repeat June values, so 2026 H1 is the latest fundamental period. Bloomberg ROE/NIM bases differ from issuer-reported figures.
  • Estimates. BI 2019/2021 "2025E" scenarios are historical expectations, not actuals. Consensus figures come from BI (Jul 2026), with contributor counts undisclosed.
  • Wallet share. The PBOC does not publish provider shares, so these are third-party estimates (Analysys, iResearch, AMRO, IMF).
